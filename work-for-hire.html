Work for Hire under Indian Copyright Law

AI-generated  ·  iPgraam IPR Knowledge Base  ·  Indian IP Law Reference

The concept of work for hire is a significant aspect of Indian copyright law, as it determines the ownership of copyright in certain circumstances. Under Indian law, the general principle is that the author of a work is the owner of the copyright, but there are exceptions to this rule. The work for hire doctrine is one such exception, which deals with the ownership of copyright in works created by employees or commissioned parties.

Overview

The work for hire doctrine is a principle of copyright law that assigns the ownership of a work to the employer or commissioning party, rather than the actual creator of the work. This doctrine is relevant in situations where a work is created by an employee as part of their job, or where a work is commissioned by a party. In India, the work for hire doctrine is governed by the Copyright Act, 1957, which provides specific provisions for determining the ownership of copyright in such cases. The doctrine is essential in balancing the rights of creators and the interests of employers or commissioning parties. According to the Copyright Act, 1957, the ownership of copyright in a work for hire is determined by the nature of the work and the relationship between the parties involved [1].

Legal Framework

The Copyright Act, 1957, is the primary legislation governing copyright law in India. Section 17 of the Act deals with the ownership of copyright, and it provides that the author of a work is the owner of the copyright, unless the work is made for hire. Section 17(b) specifically states that in the case of a work made for hire, the employer is the owner of the copyright, unless there is an agreement to the contrary. The Act also defines the term "work for hire" to include works made by employees in the course of their employment, as well as works commissioned by parties. The Indian copyright law also provides for certain exceptions and limitations to the work for hire doctrine, such as the right of authors to claim moral rights in their works [2].

Procedure

To determine the ownership of copyright in a work for hire under Indian law, the following steps can be taken: first, identify the nature of the work and the relationship between the parties involved; second, check if there is an agreement between the parties regarding the ownership of copyright; third, if no agreement exists, apply the provisions of Section 17 of the Copyright Act, 1957, to determine the ownership of copyright. In cases where the work is made by an employee, the employer is presumed to be the owner of the copyright, unless the employee can prove that the work was not made in the course of their employment. In cases where the work is commissioned, the commissioning party is presumed to be the owner of the copyright, unless the parties have agreed otherwise [3].

Key Cases

  • Eastern Book Company v. Navin J. Desai (2001) - The Delhi High Court held that the ownership of copyright in a work for hire is determined by the terms of the agreement between the parties, and in the absence of an agreement, the employer is the owner of the copyright.
  • Blackwood & Sons Ltd. v. A.N. Parasuraman (1959) - The Supreme Court of India held that the work for hire doctrine applies to works made by employees in the course of their employment, and that the employer is the owner of the copyright in such cases.
  • Mipco Seamless Rings (Gujarat) Ltd. v. Jayesh G. Shah (2006) - The Gujarat High Court held that the work for hire doctrine does not apply to works made by independent contractors, and that the ownership of copyright in such cases is determined by the terms of the agreement between the parties.
