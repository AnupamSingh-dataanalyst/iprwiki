Compulsory Licensing of Patents in India

AI-generated  ·  iPgraam IPR Knowledge Base  ·  Indian IP Law Reference

Compulsory licensing of patents in India is a provision under the Indian Patents Act, 1970, which allows the government to grant permission to a third party to manufacture and sell a patented product without the consent of the patent holder under certain circumstances. This provision is intended to ensure that patented inventions are worked in India and are available to the public at reasonable prices. The compulsory licensing provision has been used in India to make available essential medicines, particularly for the treatment of diseases such as HIV/AIDS and cancer.

Overview

The concept of compulsory licensing is based on the principle that patent rights are not absolute and are subject to certain limitations and exceptions. In India, compulsory licensing is governed by Chapter XVI of the Patents Act, 1970, which sets out the conditions under which a compulsory license can be granted. The Act provides that a compulsory license can be granted if the patented invention is not worked in India, or if the reasonable requirements of the public with respect to the patented invention are not satisfied. The Act also provides that a compulsory license can be granted in cases of national emergency or extreme urgency.

Legal Framework

The legal framework for compulsory licensing in India is provided by the Patents Act, 1970, specifically Sections 84 to 92. Section 84 of the Act provides that a compulsory license can be granted if the reasonable requirements of the public with respect to the patented invention are not satisfied, or if the patented invention is not worked in India. Section 85 of the Act provides that a compulsory license can be granted in cases of national emergency or extreme urgency. The Rules governing compulsory licensing are provided by the Patents Rules, 2003, specifically Rules 96 to 103. [1] The Patents Act, 1970, has been amended several times to incorporate provisions related to compulsory licensing, including the amendment made in 2005 to include provisions related to public health.

Procedure

The procedure for obtaining a compulsory license in India involves filing a petition with the Controller General of Patents, Designs and Trademarks. The petition must be accompanied by a statement setting out the grounds on which the petition is made, including the facts and circumstances that justify the grant of a compulsory license. The petition must also be accompanied by a fee, which is currently Rs. 60,000. The Controller may require the petitioner to provide additional information or evidence, and may also hear the patent holder before making a decision. If the Controller grants the compulsory license, the patent holder has the right to appeal to the Intellectual Property Appellate Board.

Key Cases

  • Bayer Corporation vs. Union of India (2014) - The Delhi High Court upheld the grant of a compulsory license to Natco Pharma to manufacture and sell a generic version of Bayer's cancer drug Nexavar. [2]
  • Merck vs. Glenmark (2015) - The Bombay High Court refused a compulsory license to Glenmark to manufacture and sell a generic version of Merck's diabetes drug Januvia. [3]
  • Pfizer vs. Cipla (2013) - The Delhi High Court granted a compulsory license to Cipla to manufacture and sell a generic version of Pfizer's pneumonia vaccine Prevnar. [4]
